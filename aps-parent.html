First drive: the new luxury sedan that wants to redefine the daily commute

A quieter cabin, sharper handling and a surprisingly frugal powertrain make a strong case for the segment's comeback.

By Alex Morgan 25 Nov 2026 6 min read fXin✉

Executive sedans have spent the past decade fighting for attention against a wave of SUVs. This latest model doesn't try to out-shout them — instead it doubles down on what made the class great: refinement, poise, and a sense of occasion every time you open the door.

Over two days and roughly 600 kilometres, we drove it through city traffic, winding mountain roads and long motorway stretches to find out whether it lives up to the hype.

Design

From the outside, the changes are more evolutionary than revolutionary. The grille is slimmer, the headlights sharper, and a new full-width light bar spans the boot lid. It's handsome rather than dramatic — the kind of design that should age well over a typical four-year ownership cycle.

Look closer and the details reward attention. Flush door handles pop out as you approach, the side mirrors have been reshaped to cut wind noise, and the wheel designs have been optimised for aerodynamics without looking like dinner plates.

The drag coefficient has fallen to 0.23, which the engineers say is worth around 20 km of extra electric range on a motorway run. It's also visibly lower and wider than before, with a stance that finally matches its price tag.

Technology

The infotainment system runs on a new operating system that boots almost instantly and responds without lag. Wireless smartphone mirroring is standard, and over-the-air updates mean features can be added long after you drive off the forecourt.

Driver-assistance systems have been upgraded too. Adaptive cruise control now handles stop-start traffic more smoothly, and the lane-centring assistant no longer ping-pongs between the white lines on gently curving roads.

A head-up display projects navigation arrows onto the road ahead, and an optional augmented-reality mode overlays turn instructions on the live camera feed. It sounds gimmicky, but in an unfamiliar city it genuinely helps.

The audio system deserves a special mention. Eighteen speakers, carefully tuned to the cabin's acoustics, deliver a crisp, balanced sound that rivals dedicated home systems — and thanks to the cabin's quietness, you can actually hear it.

Inside the cabin

The interior is a clear step forward. A single curved display spans most of the dash, yet physical controls remain for climate and volume — a welcome concession. Materials feel expensive where your hands land, and rear-seat space is generous enough for adults on long trips.

“It's the rare car that feels calmer the faster you go.”

Road and wind noise are impressively suppressed, helped by acoustic glazing and a revised door seal design. At motorway speed, conversation never needs to rise above a normal speaking voice.

On the road

Steering is light around town but builds weight naturally as speeds rise. The adaptive suspension soaks up broken surfaces without the float that plagued its predecessor, and body control on twisting roads is genuinely impressive for a car of this size.

The plug-in hybrid powertrain delivers a usable electric-only range for most commutes, switching seamlessly to petrol when needed. Over our mixed test loop we averaged better economy than the official figure suggests.

Running costs

Servicing intervals have been stretched to every 30,000 km or two years, and the manufacturer's fixed-price plan keeps budgeting simple. Insurance groups are broadly in line with rivals, while the plug-in hybrid's low official emissions keep company-car tax pleasantly modest.

Charge at home overnight and most weekday commutes can be done without burning a drop of fuel. On longer trips, expect real-world economy in the mid-40s mpg once the battery is depleted.

Verdict

If you've written off sedans, this might change your mind. It's comfortable, efficient and quietly confident — exactly what a daily driver should be.
